# Landing Page Copy Reference

## Overview

This reference provides a framework for SaaS landing-page copy. It covers headline formulas, messaging frameworks (PAS, AIDA, StoryBrand, JTBD, BAB, FAB), benefit/feature writing, objection handling, CTA text, and reading-level review.

Numeric conversion lifts below come from individual studies or benchmarks with specific audiences and implementations. Treat them as prompts for hypotheses, not universal forecasts. Verify the source and context before citing a figure, and test choices against product evidence.

The core copywriting principle: **every word on the page must either build desire or remove doubt.** Copy that does neither is dead weight that dilutes the visitor's attention and slows them down.

## Headlines

The headline is the single most-read element on any page. For SaaS, it must accomplish three things simultaneously: communicate what the product does, promise a specific benefit, and spark enough curiosity to keep reading.

### Constraints

- **Under 8 words / 44 characters** — brevity forces clarity. One company replaced a 14-word jargon-heavy headline with a 6-word statement and saw 27% more demo clicks in nine days.
- **Specificity over cleverness** — vague claims like "The future of productivity" fail the 5-second test. Specific outcomes like "Write emails 10x faster" pass instantly.
- **The grunt test** (Donald Miller): Could someone glance at your page and immediately understand who you are, what you offer, and how to get it? If not, rewrite.

### Proven headline formulas

**Outcome + Enabler**
Format: "{Achieve desirable outcome} with {what your product enables}"
Example: "Close more deals with AI-powered follow-ups"
Best for: Products where the outcome is the primary selling point.

**Benefit Minus Pain**
Format: "{Desired outcome} without {pain point}"
Example: "Beautiful designs without the learning curve"
Best for: Products that eliminate a known frustration. Simultaneously promises value and addresses an objection.

**Stat-Driven**
Format: Lead with a specific number or metric.
Example: "Recover 21% of abandoned carts" (outperforms "Recover more abandoned carts")
Best for: Products with quantifiable results. Numbers build instant credibility.

**Input-to-Output**
Format: "Turn {input} into {valuable output}"
Example: "Turn meeting notes into action items"
Best for: Products that transform something the user already has into something more valuable. Implies minimal effort for maximum result.

**Category for Persona**
Format: "The {category} for {specific audience}"
Example: "The CRM for solo founders"
Best for: Products targeting a well-defined niche. Immediately positions the product and makes the right audience feel seen.

**How-to / Action**
Format: "{Verb} your {thing} in {timeframe/manner}"
Example: "Launch your online store in 10 minutes"
Best for: Products where speed or ease of getting started is the key differentiator.

### Subheadline guidance

The subheadline expands on the headline's promise in one sentence. It should either:

- **Add specificity**: If the headline is aspirational, the subheadline explains how. "Beautiful designs without the learning curve" → "Drag-and-drop templates that anyone can customize in minutes."
- **Kill the top objection**: If the headline makes a bold claim, the subheadline addresses skepticism. "Recover 21% of abandoned carts" → "Automated email sequences that run while you sleep — no coding required."
- **Name the audience**: If the headline is broad, the subheadline narrows it. "The simplest way to manage projects" → "Built for freelancers and small teams who hate complexity."

### Headlines for new/unknown products

When the product or category is unfamiliar to the audience:

- Lead with the problem or outcome, not the product name or category.
- Avoid invented jargon or category names the audience doesn't recognize.
- "Stop losing customers to slow support" is better than "AI-powered customer retention platform" for an audience that doesn't know the category exists.

## Messaging Frameworks

Six frameworks serve different strategic purposes. The most effective pages often combine frameworks — using JTBD insights to inform language, PAS or AIDA to structure narrative flow, and StoryBrand principles to maintain customer-centricity throughout.

### PAS (Problem → Agitate → Solution)

Best for: Products solving a clear, felt pain point. Especially effective for B2C SaaS because consumer decisions are emotion-driven, and amplifying a felt pain creates urgency.

**How to use it:**

1. **Problem** — Name the specific frustration in the customer's own language. Be concrete, not abstract. "You spend hours every week manually updating spreadsheets" is better than "Data management is inefficient."
2. **Agitate** — Deepen the emotional tension. Describe the consequences and negative feelings without overdramatizing. What does this problem cost them in time, money, stress, or missed opportunities? "Meanwhile, your competitors are making decisions with real-time data while you're copy-pasting between tabs."
3. **Solution** — Connect your product directly to the agitated problem. The transition should feel like relief. "Product X syncs your data automatically, so you always have the full picture — without touching a spreadsheet."

**Mapping to page sections:**
- Problem → Problem statement section
- Agitate → Expanded problem or "Why this matters" subsection
- Solution → How it works + features sections

**Pitfalls to avoid:**
- Don't invent pain the audience doesn't actually feel — it reads as manipulative.
- Don't over-agitate. The goal is recognition ("yes, that's exactly my problem"), not anxiety.
- Don't rush the agitation — spend enough time on it that the solution feels earned.

### AIDA (Attention → Interest → Desire → Action)

Best for: Aspiration-driven products (fitness apps, creative tools, learning platforms) where the audience isn't necessarily in pain but wants improvement.

**How to use it:**

1. **Attention** — A bold headline or visual that stops the scroll. Can be a surprising stat, a provocative question, or a vivid image of the desired outcome.
2. **Interest** — Build curiosity by showing how the product works or sharing unexpected insights. Features, demo videos, and "how it works" sections serve this purpose.
3. **Desire** — Transform interest into wanting through testimonials, before/after comparisons, and benefit-rich descriptions. Make the visitor imagine their life with the product.
4. **Action** — Clear, compelling CTA with friction-reducing microcopy.

**Mapping to page sections:**
- Attention → Hero section
- Interest → How it works + features
- Desire → Testimonials + social proof + use cases
- Action → CTA sections throughout

### StoryBrand (Donald Miller's 7-Part Framework)

Best for: Any product where the messaging has become cluttered, jargon-heavy, or company-centric. StoryBrand's central mantra is "When you confuse, you lose." One practitioner condensed 1,000 words to under 200 using StoryBrand, and revenue jumped from $28,000 to $103,000 on the same audience.

**The 7 elements:**

1. **A Character** — The customer (not your company) is the hero. Identify who they are.
2. **Has a Problem** — Three layers:
   - External (the practical challenge): "I can't keep track of my expenses"
   - Internal (how it makes them feel): "I feel disorganized and out of control"
   - Philosophical (why it's wrong this problem exists): "Managing money shouldn't require an accounting degree"
3. **Meets a Guide** — Your product/brand, positioned as the experienced mentor (not the hero). Show empathy ("We know how frustrating this is") and authority ("Trusted by 50,000 users").
4. **Who Gives Them a Plan** — A clear, simple process (ideally 3 steps) that makes the path forward obvious and safe.
5. **And Calls Them to Action** — Direct CTA ("Start Your Free Trial") plus a transitional CTA for those not ready ("Watch a 2-minute demo").
6. **That Helps Them Avoid Failure** — Paint the stakes of inaction. What happens if they don't solve this problem?
7. **And Ends in Success** — Paint the transformation. What does life look like after they use your product?

**Key insight for landing pages:** Most SaaS pages talk about themselves ("We built the most advanced..."). StoryBrand flips this — every sentence should be about the customer's journey from problem to success, with your product as the tool that gets them there.

### JTBD (Jobs to Be Done)

Best for: Informing the *language* of your copy rather than the structure. JTBD surfaces the exact words and struggling moments real customers experience, which become the raw material for headlines, subheadlines, and benefit descriptions. JTBD-based ad copy has shown statistically significant improvements in click-through rates, conversion rates, and cost per acquisition.

**The outcome statement formula:**
"When I [situation], help me [job to be done], so I can [desired outcome]"

Example: "When I'm juggling multiple client projects, help me see all deadlines in one place, so I can deliver on time without working weekends."

**How to mine JTBD insights:**

- Interview 5–10 recent customers about their *switching moment* — what were they doing before, what triggered them to look for a solution, what made them choose your product?
- Read customer reviews (yours and competitors') for recurring language patterns.
- Look for "struggling moments" — the specific situations where the old way of doing things broke down.
- Use their exact words in your copy. Customer language always outperforms marketing language.

**When you don't have customer data yet:** Use competitor reviews, Reddit/forum discussions, and social media to find the language your target audience uses to describe the problem you solve.

### BAB (Before → After → Bridge)

Best for: Aspirational B2C products where the transformation is the primary selling point.

1. **Before** — Describe the current painful or unsatisfying state.
2. **After** — Paint the desired future state in vivid, specific terms.
3. **Bridge** — Your product is the bridge between the two.

Works especially well in hero sections and email sequences driving traffic to landing pages.

### FAB (Feature → Advantage → Benefit)

Best for: Translating product capabilities into customer-centric language. Use FAB to write individual feature sections on the page.

1. **Feature** — What the product has or does. "AI-powered scheduling."
2. **Advantage** — What that feature enables practically. "Automatically finds the best meeting time across time zones."
3. **Benefit** — The emotional or life-improvement payoff. "Never waste 10 minutes on back-and-forth emails again."

## Benefits vs. Features: Getting the Balance Right

The conventional "focus on benefits over features" advice is an oversimplification. Pages stuffed with vague benefits ("save time and money") fail because they never explain what the product actually *does*. Pages listing only features fail because visitors can't see why they should care.

### The "So What?" ladder

Start with a feature and ask "So what?" repeatedly until you reach an emotional payoff:

- Feature: "256-bit AES encryption"
- So what? → "Your data is protected against breaches"
- So what? → "You never have to worry about your personal information being exposed"
- So what? → "Peace of mind" ← This is the real benefit.

### Where benefits and features go on the page

- **Above the fold and in headlines**: Benefits dominate. Lead with outcomes and feelings.
- **Mid-page feature sections**: Each section leads with the benefit headline, then supports it with the specific feature that delivers it.
- **Below the fold for engaged visitors**: More feature detail for people actively evaluating and comparing options.

This mirrors how consumers actually decide: emotional attraction first, rational justification second.

### Writing feature sections

For each of 3–6 core features, use this pattern:

1. **Benefit headline**: What the user gains. "Never miss a deadline again"
2. **One-sentence explanation**: How the feature delivers that benefit. "Smart reminders that adapt to your work patterns and escalate as deadlines approach."
3. **Supporting visual**: Screenshot or illustration showing the feature in action.

Avoid: listing features without context, using internal product terminology, or describing implementation details ("built with React" is irrelevant to the buyer).

## Emotional vs. Rational Messaging

Harvard professor Gerald Zaltman's neuroscience research shows 95% of purchasing decisions happen in the subconscious mind, driven by emotion. The IPA Databank study found emotional campaigns outperformed rational ones on every metric — sales volume, market share, pricing power, and customer loyalty.

### The emotional-rational sequence

For B2C SaaS, the optimal pattern is:

1. **Lead with emotion** in headlines and the hero section — the feeling of relief, excitement, belonging, or empowerment the product delivers.
2. **Support with rational proof** in mid-page sections — specific features, data, comparisons, social proof — to give the logical mind permission to act on the emotional impulse.
3. **Close with emotion** in the final CTA section — remind the visitor what they'll feel after taking action.

### Key emotional triggers for consumer SaaS

- **Relief** — from frustration, overwhelm, or tedium
- **Confidence** — feeling capable and in control
- **Belonging** — joining a community of people like them
- **Pride** — achieving something impressive
- **FOMO** — not wanting to be left behind
- **Delight** — the product is genuinely enjoyable to use

Positive emotional framing generally outperforms fear-based messaging, though trust is consistently one of the most powerful emotions to build toward.

### B2C vs. B2B emotional messaging

- **B2C**: Emotional messaging can be more overt. Headlines can be aspirational, playful, or provocative. Personal desire, convenience, and delight are primary motivators.
- **B2B**: Emotion still matters (buyers are still humans), but it's channeled through professional concerns — looking competent to their boss, reducing career risk, being seen as innovative. Rational proof (ROI, case studies) carries more explicit weight.

## Objection Handling in Copy

The best-converting pages anticipate and neutralize objections before they fully form. Joe Sugarman's principle: resolve objections within the copy so visitors never leave to research elsewhere.

### The six most common B2C SaaS objections

| Objection | Where to address it | How to address it |
|-----------|-------------------|------------------|
| "Is it worth the price?" | Near pricing section | ROI framing, cost-per-day breakdowns, value comparisons ("Less than a coffee a day"), money-back guarantee |
| "Can I trust this company?" | Throughout the page | Layered social proof, review badges, media mentions, security badges, real team photos |
| "Is it easy to use?" | How-it-works section | "No coding required" reframes, 3-step process, time-to-value messaging ("Set up in 5 minutes") |
| "What about switching costs?" | Feature or FAQ section | "White-glove migration" offers, import tools, onboarding support messaging |
| "Will it work for me specifically?" | Testimonials section | Persona-matched testimonials from people like the reader |
| "What if I don't like it?" | Near every CTA | Free trials, money-back guarantees, "Cancel anytime" — pure risk reversal |

### Inline objection handling

Objections should be addressed at the point of maximum relevance, not collected in a single FAQ:

- **Price anxiety** → addressed near pricing
- **Trust concerns** → social proof woven throughout the page
- **Commitment fear** → "No credit card required" directly beneath every CTA
- **Complexity concerns** → "Set up in 5 minutes" near the how-it-works section
- **FAQ section** → serves as a safety net for remaining questions, not the primary objection-handling mechanism

### Handling objections for new products

When you're new and can't lean on large user numbers or enterprise logos:

- **Lean into risk reversal**: Generous free trials, money-back guarantees, no credit card required. If you can't prove others trust you, reduce the risk of trusting you.
- **Show the product working**: Interactive demos, video walkthroughs, and real screenshots prove capability better than any testimonial.
- **Founder story**: A brief, authentic explanation of why you built this product. Personal stories build connection when brand credibility hasn't been established yet.
- **Specificity as credibility**: Vague claims ("We're the best") need external validation. Specific claims ("Our algorithm analyzes 47 data points per lead") carry inherent credibility because they demonstrate genuine expertise.
- **Transparency**: Share your roadmap, your methodology, or your metrics openly. Transparency signals confidence.
- **Comparison framing**: Position against the status quo ("spreadsheets", "doing it manually") rather than against established competitors you can't yet credibly rival.

## Marketing CTA Text

For product-interface labels, errors, empty states, and general microcopy, follow the main ux-copy skill. This section applies only to landing and marketing experiments.

Small text changes yield surprisingly large results. These are among the highest-ROI copywriting optimizations.

### CTA button text principles

- **2–4 words** is a useful CTA starting range when it can still name the outcome clearly.
- **First-person phrasing** can outperform second-person in marketing experiments: "Start My Free Trial" creates psychological ownership. Test it against the ux-copy default rather than treating it as universal.
- **Benefit-specific text** should explain what happens; test it against generic labels rather than assuming a fixed lift.
- **"Free"** can reduce friction only when the offer is genuinely free and its terms are clear.
- **Personalized CTAs** must reflect real visitor context, preserve privacy, and be tested against a stable default.

### High-converting SaaS CTA phrases

Ranked by commonality among top-performing SaaS sites:

1. "Get Started Free" — the most common CTA in SaaS, and effective as a default
2. "Start My Free Trial"
3. "Try It Free"
4. "Create Free Account"
5. Benefit-specific variants: "Get free clips every month", "Start saving time today"

### CTA text to avoid

- "Submit" — generic and implies the user is giving something up
- "Click Here" — says nothing about the value received
- "Buy Now" — too committal for SaaS; better for e-commerce
- "Sign Up" — frames the action as work rather than gain
- "Learn More" — acceptable as a secondary CTA but too passive for primary

### Microcopy examples that moved the needle

- Changing "Register" to "Get your free account" → 28% conversion lift
- Adding "there will be no additional costs" near checkout → 11.3% increase (Yoast)
- Updated onboarding microcopy → 30% jump in user retention within one week

### Where conversion microcopy matters most

- Beneath CTA buttons: risk reversal such as "No credit card required" or "Cancel anytime"
- Around forms: explain why information is needed and what happens next
- Near pricing: clarify billing, cancellation, trial, and guarantee terms

## Reading Level and Copy Length

### Reading level

Some SaaS benchmarks associate simpler reading levels with higher conversion, but audience expertise and task complexity matter. Optimize for comprehension, not a universal grade-level or conversion multiplier.

**Practical guidelines:**

- Use short sentences (under 20 words average).
- Use common words. Replace "utilize" with "use", "facilitate" with "help", "leverage" with "use."
- Eliminate jargon unless your audience genuinely uses it daily.
- Write like you speak. Read your copy aloud — if it sounds unnatural, simplify it.
- One idea per sentence. One theme per paragraph.
- Use the Hemingway Editor or similar tools to check grade level.

### Copy length

- Optimal range for SaaS: 250–725 words at the median conversion rate.
- Simple, low-cost products convert better with shorter pages.
- Complex or higher-priced products benefit from longer pages (up to 220% more leads).
- Attention spans average 47 seconds (down from 2.5 minutes in 2004), and users read only ~20% of page text.

**The principle is information sufficiency**: include exactly enough to persuade, and no more. Every sentence should either build desire or remove doubt. If it does neither, cut it.

## Section-by-Section Copy Templates

Use these as starting frameworks, then customize for voice and product.

### Hero section

```
Headline: [Outcome] + [Enabler/Differentiator] (under 8 words)
Subheadline: [Expand the promise or kill the top objection] (1 sentence)
CTA: [Action verb] + [Benefit] + [Free/Easy qualifier]
Micro social proof: [Quantity or quality proof] (1 line)
```

Example:
```
Headline: Ship faster with AI code review
Subheadline: Catch bugs before your users do — in every pull request, automatically.
CTA: Start Free Trial
Micro social proof: Trusted by 12,000+ developers
```

### Problem section

```
Headline: [Name the frustration in the customer's words]
Body: [2–3 sentences expanding on the pain, consequences, and emotional cost]
Transition: [Bridge to your solution]
```

### How it works

```
Headline: How it works (or a benefit-framed alternative like "Get started in 3 steps")
Step 1: [Simple action] — [What happens / what the user gets]
Step 2: [Simple action] — [What happens / what the user gets]
Step 3: [Simple action] — [The payoff / transformation]
CTA: [Repeat primary CTA]
```

### Feature section (repeat for each feature)

```
Benefit headline: [What the user gains from this feature]
Body: [1–2 sentences explaining how the feature delivers the benefit]
Visual: [Screenshot or illustration of the feature]
```

### Testimonial section

```
Headline: [Social proof headline — "Loved by 50,000+ teams" or "Don't take our word for it"]
Testimonials: [2–4 testimonials with: specific result, person's name, photo, role/company]
```

### Final CTA section

```
Headline: [Restate the core benefit or paint the success state]
Subheadline: [Address final hesitation or reinforce social proof]
CTA: [Same primary CTA as hero]
Microcopy: [Risk reversal — "No credit card required. Cancel anytime."]
```

## Voice and Tone for B2C SaaS

### General principles

- **Conversational, not corporate**: Write like a knowledgeable friend, not a press release.
- **Confident, not arrogant**: "We built this to solve X" rather than "We're the world's leading..."
- **Specific, not vague**: "Save 5 hours a week" rather than "Save time."
- **Active voice**: "Our AI analyzes your data" rather than "Your data is analyzed by our AI."
- **Second person**: "You" and "your" — the page is about the visitor, not the company.

### Adapting voice to audience

- **Technical audience**: Can use more specific terminology but should still avoid unnecessary jargon. Specificity signals expertise.
- **Non-technical audience**: Focus on outcomes and analogies. Explain what, not how.
- **Mixed audience**: Lead with accessible language, offer detail for those who want it (expandable sections, "Learn more" links).

## Common Copywriting Mistakes

1. **Talking about yourself instead of the customer** — "We built..." and "Our platform..." instead of "You get..." and "Your team can..."
2. **Vague benefits** — "Save time and money" without specificity. How much time? How much money? Compared to what?
3. **Feature dumps without context** — Listing capabilities without explaining why they matter.
4. **Jargon and buzzwords** — "Leverage our best-in-class AI-powered solution" communicates nothing.
5. **Missing the emotional hook** — Leading with rational features instead of the feeling the product delivers.
6. **Ignoring objections** — Hoping visitors won't think of reasons not to buy rather than proactively addressing them.
7. **Generic CTAs** — "Submit" or "Click Here" instead of benefit-specific action text.
8. **Writing at too high a reading level** — college-level writing converts at less than half the rate of 5th–7th grade writing.
9. **Too much copy** — overwhelming the visitor with text they won't read instead of making each word earn its place.
10. **Inconsistent messaging** — the headline promises one thing, the subheadline says another, and the features section goes in a third direction. The page should tell one cohesive story.

## Copy Audit Checklist

Use this to review any landing page's copy:

- [ ] Does the headline communicate what the product does AND promise a benefit in under 8 words?
- [ ] Can someone understand the value proposition within 5 seconds?
- [ ] Is the copy written at a 5th–7th grade reading level?
- [ ] Does the page lead with benefits above the fold and support with features below?
- [ ] Is the CTA text benefit-specific (not generic "Submit" or "Sign Up")?
- [ ] Is there friction-reducing microcopy near every CTA?
- [ ] Are the top 3 objections addressed inline (not just in the FAQ)?
- [ ] Does the copy use the customer's language (not internal jargon)?
- [ ] Is the page about the customer ("you") rather than the company ("we")?
- [ ] Does the emotional hook come before the rational proof?
- [ ] Are testimonials specific and quantified (not vague praise)?
- [ ] Is every sentence either building desire or removing doubt?
